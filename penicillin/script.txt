Medicine's greatest discovery happened because a scientist didn't wash his dishes.
London, nineteen twenty-eight. Alexander Fleming was brilliant, and his lab bench was a disaster. Dishes of bacteria were stacked everywhere, some old enough to need their own name.
That summer, he went on holiday and left the whole pile behind.
When he came back, one dish had grown a fuzzy blue-green mould. Gross, but normal. Except around the mould, the bacteria were gone. Not dying. Gone. Like someone had drawn a circle and said, keep out.
Fleming grew more of the mould and tested its juice. It wiped out germs that killed people every day.
But here's the catch. He couldn't make enough of it, and almost nobody cared. For ten years, the miracle sat on a shelf.
Then a team in Oxford figured out how to brew it by the bucket. Soon factories were making millions of doses, and simple scratches stopped being deadly.
You see, the mould probably drifted in from a lab downstairs. Fleming's real genius? He looked before he scrubbed.
He won a Nobel Prize. And to this day, the most important dish in history is the one he forgot to wash.
